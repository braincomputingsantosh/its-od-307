2.1

Data Collection for AI

"Garbage in, garbage out — the data you collect determines the AI you build."

Data is the raw material of every AI system. Before a model can learn anything, you need to decide what data to collect, how to collect it, and how to verify it is good enough. This section walks through the key decisions every AI project must make at the very start.

Why Data Collection Matters

The Big Idea Think of data collection like buying ingredients before cooking. If you walk into the kitchen with the wrong ingredients — or spoiled ones — no amount of cooking skill will save the meal. AI works the same way: the best algorithm on bad data still produces bad results.

Four Key Data Characteristics

✅
Completeness
All required fields are present. Missing values create blind spots in the model.
🎯
Accuracy
Values reflect reality. A blood pressure reading of 500/300 is impossible — flag it.
🔗
Consistency
Same information stored the same way across all records and sources.
🕐
Timeliness
Data is fresh enough for the task. Yesterday's stock price can't predict today's trade.

Existing Dataset vs. Building Your Own

ApproachBest WhenWatch Out For
Use Existing Dataset
(Kaggle, UCI, EHR databases)		 You need to move fast; a public dataset covers your problem domain Data may not match your exact context; potential licensing or privacy limits
Build Custom Dataset
(surveys, sensors, scraping)		 No existing dataset fits; your problem is highly specific Expensive, time-consuming; risk of collection bias if design is poor

Automated vs. Manual Collection

Automated Collection

Speed, Scale, and Consistency

Automated tools — web scrapers, APIs, IoT sensors — collect data faster and more consistently than humans. They run 24/7 without fatigue.

  • Healthcare example: Vital-sign monitors that automatically log patient readings every 5 minutes into the EHR
  • Finance example: APIs that pull real-time stock prices and trading volumes from exchanges
  • Limitation: May collect irrelevant or low-quality data; requires technical setup and ongoing maintenance
Manual Collection

Quality, Context, and Nuance

Humans add judgment that machines cannot. Radiologists labeling X-rays or analysts reviewing earnings reports provide meaning to raw images and text.

  • Healthcare example: Radiologists marking tumor regions in mammograms so a diagnostic AI can learn from expert annotations
  • Finance example: Analysts categorizing whether a news article is positive, negative, or neutral for market sentiment analysis
  • Limitation: Slow and expensive; subject to human error and annotator disagreement

Common Pitfalls

🪤 "More Data = Better AI"
Not true. 10,000 high-quality, relevant records often outperform 1,000,000 noisy, off-topic ones.
⏳ "Perfect Data" Paralysis
Waiting until data is perfect before starting means never starting. Iterate and improve.
🤖 "Automation Assumption"
Assuming automated collection is always better — sometimes a human analyst catches context that a scraper misses entirely.

Section 2.1 — 5 Practice Questions

1. A healthcare team wants to train an AI to predict patient readmission. They find a public hospital dataset but it only covers patients aged 18–45. What data collection problem does this represent?
✔ Correct Answer: B
A dataset limited to one age group cannot produce a model that works well for older or younger patients. This is a representativeness problem — the data does not reflect the full population the AI will serve.
Teacher note: Distractor A targets confusion with automation errors. Distractor D tests whether students can distinguish representativeness from timeliness.
2. Which TWO of the following are advantages of automated data collection over manual collection? (Choose TWO)
✔ Correct Answers: A and C
Automated systems never sleep (A) and handle large volumes consistently (C). They do NOT replace human judgment for nuanced data (B), and legal/privacy compliance is still required regardless of how data is collected (D).
Teacher note: B and D are common misconceptions. Emphasize that automation solves scale and consistency, not ethical or contextual challenges.
3. A bank decides to use an existing credit-scoring dataset from a third party instead of collecting its own data. What is the MOST important risk to evaluate first?
✔ Correct Answer: B
Third-party datasets reflect someone else's customers, geography, and time period. The bank must verify the dataset matches its own customer profile before trusting predictions it generates.
Teacher note: D targets a common misconception that manual collection is inherently less reliable. Reinforce that collection method ≠ reliability.
4. Which of the following best describes the "data hoarding" problem in AI projects?
✔ Correct Answer: C
Data hoarding means collecting data "just in case" without a clear purpose. This wastes storage, increases costs, and can introduce privacy risks from holding unnecessary personal information.
Teacher note: Connect this to Section 2.4 (resource costs). Storing unused data has real financial and compliance costs.
5. A team is building an AI for diagnosing skin conditions. They plan to collect images. What should be the FIRST step in their data collection strategy?
✔ Correct Answer: B
Strategy must precede action. Defining the goal first — what conditions to detect, what labels are needed, what patient populations to cover — prevents collecting the wrong data at scale.
Teacher note: C is a common student error: algorithm selection comes AFTER understanding the data, not before. A is tempting but skips legal and quality considerations.
2.2

Data Quality Assessment

"Even the smartest detective will reach wrong conclusions with bad evidence."

Data quality assessment is the systematic process of examining data to understand its condition, reliability, and suitability for a specific AI task. Think of it as a health check-up for your data before you trust it to make real-world decisions.

The 6 Dimensions of Data Quality

📋
1. Completeness
Are all required fields filled? Missing data = blind spots in your model.
🎯
2. Accuracy
Do values reflect real-world truth? A blood pressure of 500/300 is physiologically impossible.
🔁
3. Consistency
Is the same concept recorded the same way everywhere? "Doctor" vs. "Dr." vs. "Physician" is a consistency problem.
📏
4. Validity
Does data follow business rules? A date of "February 30" is syntactically present but logically invalid.
⏱️
5. Timeliness
Is data fresh enough for the AI's purpose? 5-year-old housing prices cannot predict today's market.
🚫
6. Uniqueness
Are there duplicate records? One patient appearing twice can distort model training.

Real-World Impact of Poor Data Quality

Healthcare Example

Hospital Readmission Prediction

A hospital uses EHR data to build a readmission risk model. Quality assessment reveals:

  • Completeness: 25% of records are missing discharge diagnosis codes
  • Accuracy: Some vital signs show physiologically impossible readings
  • Consistency: Different departments use different terminology for the same conditions
  • Real-world impact: Medication errors due to poor data quality occur in ~15% of hospital admissions
Finance Example

Customer Churn Prediction

A bank builds a churn model from customer records. Quality issues found:

  • Completeness: 40% of customer records missing income data
  • Consistency: Some records use account numbers, others use email, others use phone as primary ID
  • Uniqueness: 8% of customers appear under two different IDs due to system merges
  • Real-world impact: The model targets the wrong customers, wasting retention budget

8-Step Assessment Process

1
Initial Data Exploration
Profile the data: row count, column types, value ranges, and basic statistics.
2
Completeness Assessment
Identify and quantify missing values. Which fields are critical vs. optional?
3
Accuracy Verification
Cross-check values against known ranges, external sources, or domain rules.
4
Consistency Analysis
Look for the same entity described differently across records or tables.
5
Validity Checking
Apply business rules: Are all dates valid? Are all amounts positive? Are all codes in approved lists?
6
Timeliness Evaluation
Assess how recent the data is relative to the AI's purpose.
7
Uniqueness Analysis
Detect and resolve duplicate records that could skew model training.
8
Relevance Assessment
Does this data actually contain the information needed to solve the stated problem?
Exam Practice

Section 2.2 — 5 Practice Questions

6. A dataset contains a patient record showing a birth date of "February 30, 1985." Which data quality dimension does this violate?
✔ Correct Answer: C
Validity means values follow real-world business or logic rules. February 30 is not a real date. The field is present (not a completeness issue) and not a duplicate (not uniqueness) — it's logically invalid.
Teacher note: Students often confuse validity with accuracy. Accuracy = does this reflect reality? Validity = does this follow a defined rule or constraint?
7. Which TWO actions are most important during a data quality assessment? (Choose TWO)
✔ Correct Answers: A and C
Quality assessment must quantify completeness (A) and verify accuracy through cross-validation (C). Algorithm selection (B) comes after quality assessment, and indiscriminately adding records (D) can introduce more quality problems.
Teacher note: D is the "more data always better" trap from Section 2.1. Use this as a cross-section connection point.
8. In a hospital database, the same medication "Acetaminophen" is stored as "acetaminophen," "Tylenol," and "APAP" across different departments. Which quality dimension is violated?
✔ Correct Answer: B
Consistency means the same concept is represented the same way throughout the dataset. All three names refer to the same drug, but an AI cannot know that without consistent encoding.
Teacher note: D is a common trap — students assume multiple names means inaccuracy. Emphasize: all three names are factually correct; the problem is inconsistent representation.
9. A financial firm uses 5-year-old customer transaction data to train a fraud detection model. Which data quality issue is most likely to affect model performance?
✔ Correct Answer: B
Fraud techniques evolve rapidly. A model trained on 5-year-old patterns may not recognize modern fraud methods, even if the historical data is complete and accurate.
Teacher note: Use this to introduce the concept of model drift in Section 4/5. Timeliness in training data directly feeds into why models degrade over time.
10. What is the PRIMARY purpose of setting up automated quality monitoring in a data pipeline?
✔ Correct Answer: C
Automated quality gates catch problems (missing values, outliers, format violations) at the point of entry, preventing bad data from reaching the AI model and corrupting its predictions.
Teacher note: A is a common AI-anxiety misconception. Emphasize that automation augments human oversight, it does not replace it.
2.3

Ensuring Representative Data in AI

"If you only teach a child about cats, they'll call every dog a 'strange cat.'"

Representative data means the dataset accurately reflects the full diversity of real-world situations the AI will encounter. Non-representative data produces AI systems that work well for some groups and fail — sometimes harmfully — for others.

The Three Pillars of Representative Data

🌍
Coverage
The dataset includes all relevant subgroups: age groups, genders, ethnicities, income brackets, geographic regions.
⚖️
Balance
Subgroups are proportionally represented. An 80/20 imbalance in training data means the model is optimized for the majority.
🔄
Relevance
Data reflects conditions at the time and place the AI will actually be used — not just where it was collected.

Types of Collection Bias

Bias TypeWhat HappensReal-World Example
Sampling Bias Only certain groups are included in data collection Training a skin cancer AI only on light-skinned patients — it fails on darker skin tones
Historical Bias Past data reflects past discrimination, which the AI then perpetuates Using historical loan approvals (which denied minorities more often) to train a new credit model
Measurement Bias Data is collected differently for different groups Hospital data over-represents insured patients; uninsured patients' outcomes are missing
Feedback Loop Bias The AI's decisions create new data that reinforces the original bias Predictive policing sends more officers to certain areas → more arrests there → model "confirms" those areas are high crime
Why This Is an Ethics Issue, Not Just a Technical One When AI systems fail disproportionately for underrepresented groups — denying loans, misdiagnosing conditions, flagging innocent people — the consequences fall on those groups. Representative data is not optional; it is an ethical obligation.

Case Studies

Healthcare Case Study

Pulse Oximeters and Skin Tone

Pulse oximeters — the clip-on devices that measure blood oxygen — were shown to be significantly less accurate on patients with darker skin tones. The devices were calibrated primarily on lighter-skinned subjects. When AI diagnostic models trained on this device's data were deployed in diverse hospitals, they systematically over-estimated blood oxygen levels in Black patients, delaying treatment. This is a direct result of non-representative training data.

Finance Case Study

Credit Scoring and Zip Codes

A lender trained a credit model on historical loan data. The historical data reflected decades of redlining — the practice of denying loans in certain zip codes that happened to correlate with minority neighborhoods. The AI learned that those zip codes predicted default. But the zip code was a proxy variable: it encoded race, not credit risk. The AI perpetuated discrimination while appearing to make race-neutral decisions.

Exam Practice

Section 2.3 — 5 Practice Questions

11. A hospital trains a sepsis detection AI using only data from its ICU. When deployed in the general ward, the model performs poorly. What is the MOST likely cause?
✔ Correct Answer: B
ICU patients are sicker and monitored more intensively than general ward patients. A model trained on ICU data learns patterns specific to critically ill patients and cannot generalize. The deployment context was not represented in training data.
Teacher note: This is a classic example of domain shift — the difference between training distribution and deployment distribution.
12. What is a "proxy variable" in the context of AI bias?
✔ Correct Answer: B
Zip code, last name, and school attended are examples of proxy variables. They appear neutral but can encode race, ethnicity, or socioeconomic status, allowing a model to discriminate without explicitly using protected attributes.
Teacher note: A is a plausible distractor since "proxy" sounds like a substitute. Emphasize the concept of correlation with protected characteristics.
13. Which TWO of the following best describe strategies for improving data representativeness? (Choose TWO)
✔ Correct Answers: A and C
Stratified sampling (A) ensures proportional coverage. Auditing collection processes (C) surfaces systematic gaps before they become model biases. B would worsen representativeness; D describes labeled data selection, not representativeness.
Teacher note: B tests whether students understand that removing minority data is the opposite of the solution.
14. A predictive policing AI is deployed and begins sending more officers to Neighborhood X. More arrests occur there. The model then identifies Neighborhood X as higher risk. This is an example of:
✔ Correct Answer: C
A feedback loop bias occurs when the AI's own decisions generate new training data that reinforces the original prediction. More policing → more arrests → model "confirms" the area is high-crime, regardless of actual crime rates.
Teacher note: This is one of the most powerful examples of how an AI can perpetuate injustice while appearing objective. Use it as a discussion anchor.
15. An AI company is building a loan approval model. They remove the "race" column from their dataset before training. Does this guarantee a fair model?
✔ Correct Answer: B
Simply removing the "race" column does not prevent discrimination. Other variables (zip code, name, school attended) can act as proxies and effectively re-encode race, allowing the model to discriminate by proxy.
Teacher note: A is the most common student error. This is a critical takeaway: fairness requires more than removing sensitive columns.
2.4

AI Resource Requirements & Project Feasibility

"You wouldn't drive cross-country without checking the fuel, tires, and budget first."

Many AI projects fail not because the idea was bad — but because teams never asked: "Do we actually have what it takes to build this?" Resource assessment answers that question before money is spent.

The Four Resource Dimensions

💻
Computing Resources
CPU/GPU processing power, cloud vs. on-premise infrastructure. Some models require supercomputers; others run on a laptop.
⏳
Time Complexity
How long will training take as data grows? A model that takes 2 days to train cannot support real-time decision making.
📦
Data Requirements
Volume, variety, and availability. Some problems require millions of labeled examples; others can work with hundreds.
💰
Budget & Expertise
Cloud compute, data labeling, talent, and ongoing maintenance all carry real costs that must be planned upfront.

When to Proceed vs. Pause

SignalLikely Decision
The problem is well-defined, data exists, and compute is accessible✅ Proceed
The problem is clear but training data does not exist and would be expensive to collect⚠️ Pause — assess collection cost vs. business value
The required model would take months to train on available hardware⚠️ Consider cloud compute or a simpler model
Similar AI solutions already exist and can be purchased🔄 Buy vs. Build analysis required
Regulatory requirements for the domain (healthcare, finance) are unclear🚫 Delay — compliance must be confirmed first
Real-World Example: Drug Discovery Pharmaceutical companies have invested billions in AI-driven drug discovery. The compute requirements (training on molecular structures) are enormous, timelines run years, and the regulatory path adds years more. An organization without access to specialized hardware, rare chemistry datasets, and domain experts cannot replicate this — regardless of algorithmic sophistication.
Exam Practice

Section 2.4 — 5 Practice Questions

16. A startup wants to build an AI system for real-time fraud detection. The system must process each transaction in under 100 milliseconds. Which resource dimension is MOST critical to assess first?
✔ Correct Answer: B
Real-time requirements impose strict latency constraints. If the model cannot produce a result in under 100ms, it is unusable — regardless of accuracy. This makes compute and time complexity the primary feasibility gate.
Teacher note: D is a common distractor — students often jump to algorithm selection. Emphasize: infrastructure feasibility comes before algorithm design.
17. Which TWO of the following are considered "hidden costs" that teams often underestimate in AI projects? (Choose TWO)
✔ Correct Answers: A and C
Post-deployment monitoring (A) is a recurring cost that teams frequently forget to budget. Data labeling (C) — paying humans to annotate images, text, or audio — can cost more than compute for supervised learning projects.
Teacher note: B and D are visible, obvious costs. The exam tests whether students identify the non-obvious costs.
18. A hospital AI team realizes their on-premise servers would take 6 months to train the model they need. What is the MOST practical next step?
✔ Correct Answer: C
Cloud computing allows teams to rent powerful GPU clusters for short periods, solving a hardware bottleneck without purchasing new infrastructure. A is defeatist. B may compromise model quality. D is incorrect — unsupervised vs. supervised doesn't determine training speed.
Teacher note: D tests a common misconception about learning type vs. compute requirements.
19. Which of the following best describes why "time complexity" matters for AI project feasibility?
✔ Correct Answer: B
Time complexity is a measure of how computational demands scale with data size. A model with poor time complexity may be fine on small data but prohibitively slow at scale or for real-time use.
Teacher note: D is a common student assumption. Time complexity is relevant at any scale.
20. A team wants to build an AI for detecting rare diseases. They find that only 50 confirmed cases exist in the world. What is the PRIMARY feasibility concern?
✔ Correct Answer: C
Supervised learning models typically need hundreds to thousands of labeled examples to generalize. 50 cases creates a class-imbalance problem so severe that the model may simply learn to always predict "no disease" and achieve 99.9% accuracy while being clinically useless.
Teacher note: This is a great bridge to the concept of class imbalance in Section 3 (algorithms and models).
2.5

Data Conversion for AI

"Every photo, word, and sound must be translated into numbers before AI can learn from it."

AI systems are fundamentally mathematical. They can only process numbers. Everything else — text, images, audio, video, categorical labels — must be converted into numerical form before a model can learn from it. This section covers the three core transformations that make raw data AI-ready.

The Three Pillars of Data Conversion

📐
1. Format Standardization
Convert all data into consistent, structured formats. Images must be the same size; dates must use the same format; text must use the same encoding.
01
2. Binary Representation
All information ultimately lives in computers as 0s and 1s. Images become pixel arrays; text becomes character codes; audio becomes frequency values.
🔧
3. Feature Engineering
Transform raw numbers into meaningful, model-ready variables. Raw date → day of week + is_holiday + season. Raw text → sentiment score + word count + topic category.

Feature Engineering: The Kitchen Prep Analogy

Think of it this way Feature engineering is like preparing ingredients before cooking. You don't throw whole vegetables into a blender — you wash, peel, chop, and portion them first. Similarly, raw data contains valuable information that must be extracted, reshaped, and portioned before an AI algorithm can work with it effectively.

The 4-Step Feature Engineering Process

1
Data Understanding
Examine raw data types, ranges, distributions. What information is present? What is missing?
2
Identify Transformations
Determine what changes are needed: normalization, encoding, imputation, or new feature creation.
3
Implement Transformations
Apply changes systematically and document every step. Consistency is critical — new data must be processed exactly the same way.
4
Create & Manage Processed Datasets
Store cleaned, transformed features with full documentation so any team member (or future you) can reproduce the result.

Key Transformation Techniques

TechniqueWhat It DoesExample
Normalization / Scaling Converts values to a common scale (0–1 or z-score) so large numbers don't dominate Age (0–100) and income (0–1,000,000) both scaled to 0–1
One-Hot Encoding Converts categorical text into binary columns (one column per category) "Blood type" A/B/AB/O → four columns, each 0 or 1
Missing Value Imputation Fills missing values with a calculated substitute (mean, median, predicted) Missing patient weight filled with the average weight for that age group
Temporal Feature Extraction Breaks a date/time into separate useful features "2023-03-15" → day=Wednesday, month=March, is_weekend=False, quarter=Q1
Text Vectorization Converts words into numerical vectors that capture meaning Patient feedback → sentiment score (-1 to +1) + complaint category
Critical Rule: Transformation Consistency Whatever transformation you apply to training data MUST be applied identically to all new incoming data. If you normalized ages using a 0–100 scale during training, you cannot use a 0–120 scale at prediction time. Inconsistency here causes silent, hard-to-detect model failures.
Exam Practice

Section 2.5 — 5 Practice Questions

21. A dataset contains a "blood_type" column with values A, B, AB, and O. Which transformation is most appropriate before using this data in a machine learning model?
✔ Correct Answer: B
Blood type is a categorical variable with no natural numeric order. One-hot encoding creates binary columns (A=1 or 0, B=1 or 0, etc.) without implying that AB is "greater than" A — which label encoding would incorrectly suggest.
Teacher note: D tests whether students know AI cannot process raw text. A tests confusion between normalization (for continuous numeric data) and encoding (for categorical data).
22. A model was trained using income data normalized to a 0–1 scale based on a maximum income of $200,000. A new customer submits an application with an income of $350,000. What is the MOST important action to take?
✔ Correct Answer: C
Transformation consistency requires using the original normalization parameters at inference time. The out-of-distribution value (above training range) should be flagged for review, but the transformation process itself must remain consistent.
Teacher note: D introduces a dangerous anti-pattern: re-training on every new value is operationally impossible and would cause model instability.
23. Which TWO of the following are examples of temporal feature extraction? (Choose TWO)
✔ Correct Answers: A and C
Temporal feature extraction means deriving meaningful time-based variables from raw date/time data. Day of week (A) and days since last visit (C) are examples that capture patterns a raw timestamp cannot. B is imputation; D is binary conversion — different concepts.
Teacher note: D tests whether students confuse binary representation (the underlying computer storage layer) with feature engineering (the analytical layer).
24. What is data leakage in the context of feature engineering?
✔ Correct Answer: B
Data leakage means the model "sees" the answer during training — for example, including a "post-diagnosis" column when predicting whether a patient will be diagnosed. The model performs beautifully in testing but fails completely in the real world.
Teacher note: A describes a privacy breach (relevant to ethics). C describes data drift (relevant to monitoring). Both are plausible distractors that test broader course coverage.
25. A machine learning engineer creates 200 new features through feature engineering. Model accuracy on training data is excellent but drops sharply on real-world data. What is the MOST likely cause?
✔ Correct Answer: C
Overfitting occurs when a model learns the training data too specifically — including random noise — rather than the underlying patterns. Creating hundreds of features without selection often causes this. Fewer, well-chosen features usually generalize better.
Teacher note: D is a plausible distractor. Clarify: D would cause the model to fail in a detectable, consistent way. C causes the model to appear to succeed during development before failing in production.
