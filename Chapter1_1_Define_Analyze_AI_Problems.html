Chapter 1 Objectives
Part 1 — Why This Matters
Why You Need to Learn Chapter 1
🩺 Opening Analogy — Use This to Open Class

Imagine you're a doctor. Before prescribing medicine, you diagnose the problem first. Is it a bacterial infection? A broken bone? You wouldn't give antibiotics for a fracture.

AI works exactly the same way. Before building any AI solution, you must first understand what type of problem you're solving. Get this wrong — and it's like using a hammer to perform surgery. The tool isn't wrong; you are just using it on the wrong problem.

In business terms: wasted resources, failed projects, and missed opportunities. Your book says it best — "the most expensive AI is the one you build for the wrong problem."

🌍

AI Is Everywhere Now

Whether you go into business, healthcare, or any field, you'll encounter AI systems daily. Understanding this protects you from being just a passive user — you become an informed participant.

💸

Wrong Problem = Costly Failure

The book opens with this truth: a bank once invested millions in AI to predict stock prices, only to realize they needed a simple calculator for account balances. Chapter 1 prevents this.

🔍

The Most Valuable Skill Is Knowing "When NOT to Use AI"

Anyone can ask an AI to solve something. The skill — and the certification — requires you to know when to use AI, when not to, and how to define the problem correctly.

🏥

Real Stakes in Healthcare & Finance

In healthcare, a misclassified patient can be life-threatening. In finance, a wrong fraud detection decision blocks real customers. The way you define the problem determines everything downstream.

Part 2 — Core Concepts
The 6 Critical Questions AI Problem Identification Must Answer
01

Problem Definition

What exactly are you trying to solve? Not "better customer service" — but "reduce wait times from 10 min to 2 min."

02

Need Assessment

Why does this problem matter? What happens if you don't solve it? What improves if you do?

03

Input / Output Analysis

What data do you have available (inputs)? What decisions or predictions do you want (outputs)?

04

AI Suitability

Not every problem needs AI. Some need simple rules, others need human judgment. Does this problem involve patterns, scale, or learning?

05

Cost-Benefit Analysis

What are the potential upsides (efficiency, savings)? What are the downsides (cost, errors, privacy risks)?

06

Success Metrics & Risk

What does success look like in measurable terms? What's the acceptable error rate? What's the fallback plan?

Part 3 — Real-World Examples
AI Problem Identification in Action
Healthcare

Emergency Room Patient Triage

Problem:Emergency rooms need to prioritize patients by severity, but human triage can be inconsistent during peak hours.
Inputs:Vital signs, symptoms, age, medical history, arrival time
Output:Priority level → Critical / Urgent / Standard / Non-urgent
Why AI?Pattern recognition in medical data, learns from thousands of prior triage decisions, consistent 24/7
Key Risk:Misclassification could be life-threatening — requires 95%+ accuracy and constant human oversight
✅ AI-Worthy — Clear pattern recognition, high-volume decisions, measurable outcome
Healthcare

Medication Adherence Predictor

Problem:Patients often forget medications, leading to treatment failures and costly hospitalizations.
Inputs:Patient demographics, prescription history, past reminder response rates
Output:Risk score for non-adherence + optimal reminder timing per patient
Why AI?Behavioral pattern recognition — personalization at scale that no human team could match
Success Metric:20% increase in medication adherence, 15% reduction in emergency visits
✅ AI-Worthy — Personalization at scale, strong historical data, clear measurable outcome
Finance

Credit Card Fraud Detection

Problem:Credit card companies need to flag fraudulent transactions in real-time — milliseconds matter.
Inputs:Transaction amount, location, time, merchant type, customer history, device fingerprint
Output:Fraud probability score → Approve / Decline / Flag for Review
Why AI?Learns from millions of past fraud cases, detects new patterns as fraud evolves — no human could review every transaction
Success Metric:99.9% accuracy, less than 1% false positives, sub-second decision time
✅ AI-Worthy — Real-time scale, complex evolving patterns, high ROI from automation
Finance

Personalized Savings Advisor

Problem:Bank customers need personalized financial advice, but hiring advisors for every customer isn't scalable.
Inputs:Transaction history, income, stated goals, life events, market conditions
Output:Personalized saving recommendations, spending alerts, investment suggestions
Why AI?Personalization at scale — 24/7 availability for millions of users simultaneously
Key Risk:Generic advice may not fit complex situations; privacy concerns around financial data
✅ AI-Worthy — Scale + personalization + pattern recognition in behavioral data
🚫 The "AI-Not-Needed" Red Flags (from the book)

Your book includes a clear checklist. Use these to spark class discussion:

✅ AI IS Worth Considering When…

🔵Pattern Recognition: Does the problem involve finding patterns in large amounts of data?
🔵Scale: Do you need to process more information than humans can handle efficiently?
🔵Consistency: Would consistent, repeatable decisions improve outcomes?
🔵Learning: Could the system improve by learning from more examples over time?
🔵Automation Value: Would automating this process create significant, measurable value?

🚫 AI is NOT Needed When…

🔴Simple Rules Work: Can the problem be solved with basic if-then logic? No AI needed.
🔴One-Time Task: Is this a one-off problem that won't recur at scale?
🔴Human Creativity Required: Does the solution require ethical judgment or creative thinking?
🔴Insufficient Data: Do you have fewer than hundreds of relevant examples to learn from?
Non-AI Example

Bank Needs to "Add Up Account Balances"

Request:A bank wants to use AI to total up all customer account balances at the end of each day.
Why AI Fails Here:There is no pattern to find — this is deterministic arithmetic. A spreadsheet formula does this perfectly.
Real Cost:Months of development + infrastructure + AI team salaries vs. a $0 Excel formula
🚫 Not AI-Worthy — Simple rules work, no pattern recognition needed, enormous cost for zero added value
Part 4 — Interactive Q&A
Exam-Style Questions: Section 1.1
🎯

Q 01
A hospital wants to use AI to automatically sort incoming patients in the emergency room by how urgently they need care. Which of the following BEST describes the type of AI problem this represents?
▾
  • AA regression problem, because the system predicts a continuous severity score
  • BA classification problem, because the system sorts patients into defined priority categories
  • CA reinforcement learning problem, because the system learns by trial and error
  • DAn unsupervised learning problem, because patient data has no labels
✅ Correct Answer: B
The problem asks the AI to place each patient into one of pre-defined priority categories (Critical, Urgent, Standard, Non-urgent). Any time you are sorting into known, labeled categories, that is a classification problem — the most common type of supervised learning.

Why the others are wrong:
• A is wrong — regression outputs a continuous number (like a price or temperature), not a category.
• C is wrong — reinforcement learning involves trial-and-error reward systems, like game-playing AI.
• D is wrong — unsupervised learning is used when no labels exist; here we know the categories in advance.
Ask a follow-up: "What would the inputs be?" Expected answers: vitals, symptoms, age, arrival time. This connects Input/Output Analysis (Question 3 of the 6 critical questions).
Q 02
A retail company asks its IT team to build an AI system to add up daily sales totals. The IT team recommends NOT using AI. Which reason from the AI Problem Identification framework BEST justifies this recommendation?
▾
  • AThe company does not have enough budget for AI infrastructure
  • BThe problem involves too much data for AI to process
  • CThe problem can be solved with simple rules and does not require pattern recognition
  • DAI systems cannot work with sales data
✅ Correct Answer: C
According to the AI Problem Identification framework in the book, one of the clearest "AI Not Needed" red flags is: "Can the problem be solved with if-then statements or simple arithmetic?" Adding numbers is deterministic — there's no pattern to find, no learning to be done. A spreadsheet formula handles this instantly at zero cost.

Why the others are wrong:
• A is a business constraint, not the framework reason — the framework reason is about problem type.
• B is incorrect — AI can process large amounts of data; that's actually one of its strengths.
• D is false — AI can work with any structured data, including sales figures.
This is a common exam trap — budget and technical limits sound like good reasons, but the framework-correct answer focuses on problem suitability, not resources.
Q 03
When defining an AI problem, a project manager writes: "We want to improve customer service." According to the AI Problem Identification framework, what is the PRIMARY issue with this problem statement?
▾
  • ACustomer service is not an appropriate domain for AI applications
  • BThe statement is too vague and lacks measurable, specific outcomes
  • CThe problem requires reinforcement learning, which is too advanced
  • DThe problem does not include enough input data sources
✅ Correct Answer: B
The book gives this exact example. The framework requires clear, specific, measurable problem definitions. "Improve customer service" is too broad — you cannot build an AI around it, cannot measure whether it succeeded, and cannot identify what inputs or outputs are needed.

The correct version would be: "Reduce customer wait times for simple inquiries from 10 minutes to 2 minutes." This has a clear current state, desired state, and measurable success metric.
Great class exercise: Ask students to rewrite "improve customer service" into a proper AI problem statement using the 6-question framework. Give 2 minutes, then share.
Q 04
A bank wants to build an AI system that detects fraudulent credit card transactions in real-time. Which TWO factors from the AI suitability checklist BEST justify using AI for this problem?
▾
  • AThe problem involves recognizing patterns across millions of transactions that humans cannot review manually
  • BThe bank already has AI developers on staff
  • CFraud patterns evolve constantly, so the system must learn and improve over time
  • DThe bank's competitors are also using AI for fraud detection
✅ Correct Answers: A and C
From the book's "AI-Worthy Checklist":

• A — Scale/Pattern Recognition: No human team can review millions of transactions per second. AI excels at pattern recognition at a scale humans cannot match.
• C — Learning: Fraud tactics constantly evolve, so a system that learns from new fraud examples is far more effective than static rules.

Why B and D are wrong:
• B — Having AI staff is a resource question, not a problem suitability question.
• D — Competitor behavior is a business strategy reason, not a technical justification from the framework.
Certiport exams often use "choose TWO" questions like this. Reinforce that students should filter answers through the framework, not common sense alone.
Q 05
In the AI Problem Identification framework, "Input/Output Analysis" refers to which of the following?
▾
  • AAnalyzing the power consumption of AI hardware components
  • BIdentifying what data is available to the AI system and what decisions or predictions it should produce
  • CCalculating the cost of AI implementation versus manual processes
  • DReviewing legal requirements for data storage
✅ Correct Answer: B
The book defines Input/Output Analysis as: "Identifying what information you have available (inputs) and what you want to achieve (outputs). This is like mapping the journey from Point A to Point B."

For fraud detection:
• Inputs: Transaction amount, location, time, merchant type, customer history
• Output: Fraud probability score → Approve / Decline / Review decision

This analysis is critical because without knowing your inputs and outputs clearly, you cannot choose the right algorithm or measure success.
Quick classroom exercise: For the ER triage scenario, ask students to name 3 inputs and 1 output. Expected inputs: vitals, symptoms, age. Output: priority level.
Q 06
A healthcare company is building an AI model to predict which patients are at risk of missing their medications. Which success metric is MOST appropriate for evaluating this AI system?
▾
  • AThe number of AI engineers needed to maintain the system
  • BThe speed at which the AI model was trained
  • CA measurable increase in the percentage of patients taking medications as prescribed
  • DThe number of features (data points) used in the model
✅ Correct Answer: C
The book's example uses this exact scenario with the metric: "20% increase in medication adherence, 15% reduction in emergency visits."

Success metrics must be:
• Tied directly to the original business problem
• Measurable with a specific number or percentage
• Reflecting real-world outcome improvement, not technical model performance

A, B, and D measure technical or operational aspects of the AI system — not whether it actually solved the healthcare problem it was built for.
Key distinction: Model accuracy (technical metric) ≠ Business success metric. Both matter, but the problem statement must define the business metric first.
Q 07
Which of the following scenarios represents a situation where AI is NOT the appropriate solution, according to the "AI-Not-Needed Red Flags" in the book?
▾
  • AA hospital wants to identify early signs of sepsis by analyzing thousands of patient vital signs per minute
  • BA streaming service wants to recommend movies based on a user's past viewing history
  • CA small business wants to automatically send a "thank you" email whenever a customer makes a purchase
  • DA bank wants to detect unusual account activity that may indicate money laundering
✅ Correct Answer: C
Sending a "thank you" email when a purchase is made is a simple if-then rule: IF purchase = true, THEN send email. This is deterministic — no pattern recognition, no learning, no ambiguity. Any basic automation tool (not AI) handles this perfectly.

The other three are legitimate AI use cases:
• A — Pattern recognition in high-speed, high-volume medical data
• B — Personalized recommendations from behavioral patterns
• D — Anomaly detection in financial transaction patterns
Ask students: "What tool WOULD you use for the thank-you email?" Expected answers: Mailchimp automation, website trigger, CRM workflow. This reinforces that AI is a tool, not a default solution.
Q 08
A fintech company wants to predict whether a loan applicant will repay their loan. According to the AI Problem Identification framework, what is the FIRST step in properly scoping this problem?
▾
  • ASelect the most accurate machine learning algorithm available
  • BCollect as much customer data as possible before defining the problem
  • CWrite a clear, specific problem statement that defines what the AI should predict and how success will be measured
  • DCalculate the total budget available for the AI project
✅ Correct Answer: C
The first of the 6 critical questions is always Problem Definition. Before choosing algorithms, collecting data, or budgeting, you must be crystal clear on what you are trying to achieve.

For this scenario, a proper problem statement would be:
"Predict whether a loan applicant will repay within 24 months based on credit history, income, and debt-to-income ratio, with a false positive rate below 5%."

Without this, teams collect the wrong data, build the wrong model, and measure the wrong outcomes — leading to expensive failures.
Exam pattern: "FIRST step" or "PRIMARY reason" questions always point back to the foundational framework concept — in this case, Problem Definition.
Q 09
In an AI project, a "Cost-Benefit Analysis" is BEST described as which of the following?
▾
  • AA technical evaluation of which algorithm runs fastest on available hardware
  • BAn assessment of the potential benefits AI could deliver against the risks, costs, and downsides of building it
  • CA comparison of how much different cloud providers charge for AI computing
  • DA review of how much the AI dataset costs to purchase
✅ Correct Answer: B
The book defines Cost-Benefit Analysis as: "Understanding both the potential upsides (efficiency, accuracy, cost savings) and downsides (implementation costs, potential errors, privacy concerns)."

This is not just about money — it includes:
• Benefits: Efficiency gains, revenue improvement, error reduction
• Costs: Development time, infrastructure, maintenance, staff training
• Risks: Privacy concerns, potential for bias, regulatory issues, system failures

All six must be weighed before approving an AI project.
Class discussion prompt: "Can you think of an AI project where the risks outweigh the benefits?" Example: An AI that recommends medical treatments without a doctor review — benefits are speed, risk is misdiagnosis.
Q 10
A project team has defined its AI problem, analyzed inputs and outputs, and confirmed AI is suitable. They now want to ensure the project doesn't fail at deployment. According to the framework, which element should they define NEXT?
▾
  • AThe color scheme and dashboard design for the AI's user interface
  • BThe number of employees who will use the system daily
  • CClear success metrics and a risk management plan, including fallback procedures if the AI fails
  • DThe brand name and logo for the AI product
✅ Correct Answer: C
The sixth and final critical question in the AI Problem Identification framework is Success Metrics & Risk Management: defining what success looks like in measurable terms and identifying potential pitfalls.

The book's example for the coffee shop AI states: "Risk benchmark: System should be 95% accurate or fall back to human scheduling."

This is essential because without pre-defined success metrics, you cannot tell whether the project succeeded — and without a risk/fallback plan, a system failure has no recovery path.
Reinforce: This is Question 6 of the 6 critical questions. A good study technique is having students memorize all 6 in order. Consider creating a mnemonic together as a class.
